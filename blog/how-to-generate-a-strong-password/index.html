How to Generate a Strong Password (and Actually Remember It)

September 14, 2026

Most advice about passwords focuses on the wrong thing. Forcing in a capital letter, a number and an exclamation point makes "Password1!" technically pass a strength checkbox while doing almost nothing to stop the attacks that actually break into accounts. Here's what really makes a password strong, and a way to end up with one you can actually type without staring at a sticky note.

Length beats complexity

Password strength comes down to how many guesses an attacker needs before they land on the right one, and that number grows explosively with every extra character — far faster than it grows from swapping a letter for a symbol. A random 8-character password can be cracked by modern hardware in hours; a random 16-character one would take longer than anyone alive will wait. If you only change one habit, make it this: prefer a longer password over a "clever" short one.

The real danger isn't a weak password — it's a reused one

Most account break-ins don't come from someone guessing a password character by character. They come from a breach at one website leaking its user database, and an attacker trying those same email/password pairs against every other popular site. If you use the same password on your email and on some forum that gets hacked, the forum leak is what gives someone your email. A strong password that's reused everywhere is weaker in practice than a random one used on exactly one site.

Passphrases: strong and actually memorable

If you need to remember a password yourself (rather than storing it in a password manager), string together four or five unrelated random words instead of one word with substitutions — something like correct-horse-battery-staple rather than P@ssw0rd!. Four random dictionary words is both longer and, because the words are unrelated, harder to guess than a "leetspeak" version of a common word, while being far easier for a human to memorize and type.

Let a generator do the hard part

For accounts you'll store in a password manager anyway (which should be most of them), skip memorability entirely and use a fully random password — it's strictly stronger and you never have to type it by hand. A short, practical checklist

  • Use a different password for every account that matters — email, banking, and anywhere your identity or payment info lives, at minimum.
  • Aim for 16+ characters when the site allows it; length matters more than mixing in symbols.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication wherever it's offered — it protects you even if a password does leak.
  • Use a password manager to generate and store random, unique passwords so you only have to remember one master password.
  • Never reuse your email account's password anywhere else — it's usually the key that unlocks every other "forgot password" link.
