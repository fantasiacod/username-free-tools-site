Celsius to Fahrenheit Conversion: The Formula, a Chart, and Why It's Not Just Multiplication

September 24, 2026

Converting Celsius to Fahrenheit comes up constantly — checking the weather forecast for a trip to the US, following a recipe that lists an oven temperature in Fahrenheit, or reading a thermostat in a different unit than you're used to. The formula is simple once you know it, but it looks stranger than a plain unit conversion because Celsius and Fahrenheit don't start at the same zero point — which is exactly why you can't just multiply. Here's the formula, a quick reference chart for temperatures people actually look up, and why the math works the way it does.

The Celsius to Fahrenheit formula

The formula is: °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32. Multiply the Celsius value by 9/5 (or 1.8, same thing), then add 32. For example, 20°C × 1.8 = 36, plus 32 = 68°F — a comfortable room temperature. The "× 9/5" scales for the fact that a Fahrenheit degree is a smaller unit than a Celsius degree (180 Fahrenheit degrees span the same range as 100 Celsius degrees, between freezing and boiling water), and the "+ 32" shifts for the fact that the two scales don't start at the same zero. Skip either step and the answer will be wrong, which is why "just multiply by 2" — a common shortcut — only gets you an approximate answer, never an exact one.

Common temperatures at a glance

  • Water freezes: 0°C = 32°F
  • Room temperature: about 20°C = 68°F
  • Average human body temperature: 37°C = 98.6°F
  • A hot summer day: 35°C = 95°F
  • Moderate baking oven: 180°C = 356°F
  • Water boils (at sea level): 100°C = 212°F

Converting the other direction: Fahrenheit to Celsius

Going the other way just reverses the two steps: subtract 32 first, then multiply by 5/9 (the inverse of 9/5). The formula is °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9. So 68°F − 32 = 36, times 5/9 = 20°C. It's easy to mix up which operation comes first in which direction — a safe rule of thumb: converting to Fahrenheit, multiply then add; converting to Celsius, subtract then multiply.

Why they don't share a zero point

Celsius was designed around water: 0° is where it freezes, 100° is where it boils at sea level, a clean decimal scale. Fahrenheit predates that system — it was originally based on a mix of a brine solution's freezing point and (roughly) human body temperature, set by Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit in the early 1700s, decades before Celsius existed. That history is why the two scales' zero points land in different places, and why converting between them needs both a multiplication and an addition, not just one or the other. Kelvin, the third scale scientists use, sidesteps the whole problem by starting at absolute zero — 0K is the coldest temperature theoretically possible — so it needs no negative numbers at all, just a fixed offset from Celsius (K = °C + 273.15).

Quick reference

  • °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32; °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9.
  • Multiplying by 2 and adding 30 gives a rough Fahrenheit estimate, but it's not exact — use the real formula when it matters.
  • Water's freezing (32°F) and boiling (212°F) points are 180 Fahrenheit degrees apart, versus 100 Celsius degrees for the same range — that's where the 9/5 factor comes from.
  • Kelvin shares its degree size with Celsius but starts at absolute zero, so K = °C + 273.15.
